Genes and protein domains: Prodigal and HMMER

A new bacterial genome is a long string of letters. Prodigal finds the genes in it that code for proteins. HMMER then compares proteins – or DNA – with profile HMMs: models of a whole family, built from an alignment of its members, which find relatives that a search with a single sequence can miss.

The examples work in a folder of your own:

Prodigal: the genes of a bacterium

/data/examples/bacteria/ has the first 60,000 bases of the chromosomes of Escherichia coli K-12 and Salmonella enterica Typhimurium, from NCBI RefSeq.

prodigal -i /data/examples/bacteria/ecoli.fna -o ecoli.gff -f gff -a ecoli.faa -d ecoli.ffn
head -5 ecoli.gff
grep -c '>' ecoli.faa

Prodigal first learns the genome – how rich in G and C its coding regions are, which start codons and ribosome binding sites it uses – and then finds its genes. It warns that it would like 100,000 bases or more to learn from; 60,000 are enough to see how it works. It finds 56 genes. A line of ecoli.gff is one gene: the sequence, the program, the type (CDS, coding sequence), start and end, a score, the strand, and in the last column what Prodigal knows about it – partial (10: it runs off the left end of the sequence), the start codon, the ribosome binding site (rbs_motif) and its confidence. The second gene, from 337 to 2,799 on the + strand, is thrA, just where the annotation of the genome has it. The first runs off the start of the window, so Prodigal cannot know where it begins.

ecoli.faa holds the proteins and ecoli.ffn the genes' DNA, one record per gene, named after the sequence and the gene's number:

head -2 ecoli.faa
prodigal -i /data/examples/bacteria/salmonella.fna -o salmonella.gff -f gff -a salmonella.faa -q
prodigal -p meta -i /data/examples/bacteria/combined.fna -o meta.gff -f gff -q
grep -c CDS ecoli.gff salmonella.gff meta.gff

-q keeps Prodigal quiet but for warnings. -p meta does not learn: it uses 50 models that come with Prodigal, for genomes of different GC content – right for a metagenome, where the contigs come from many organisms, and for sequences too short to learn from. combined.fna holds both windows. Other options: -c (no genes running off the ends), -g 4 (another genetic code: 11 is that of bacteria and archaea), -s FILE (every possible start, with its scores), -t FILE (save what it learnt, and use it again).

HMMER: a profile from an alignment

/data/examples/hmmer/ holds the tutorial files of HMMER 3.4, under their own names, so the tutorial of the HMMER User's Guide can be followed here as it is written. First a profile of the globins, built from an alignment of four of them, and a search of 45 globins with it:

hmmbuild globins4.hmm /data/examples/hmmer/globins4.sto
hmmsearch globins4.hmm /data/examples/hmmer/globins45.fa > globins.out
head -24 globins.out

The profile has 149 match states (M=149): for each column of the alignment, how likely each amino acid is there, and how likely a gap. hmmsearch ranks the sequences by E-value – the number of hits this good that a search of a database of this size would give by chance – and its score in bits. All 45 globins are found, with E-values of 1e-36 or smaller, although the profile was built from only four. The output then shows each sequence's domains and their alignments to the profile. For tables, one line per sequence and one per domain:

hmmsearch --tblout globins.tbl --domtblout globins.domtbl --noali globins4.hmm /data/examples/hmmer/globins45.fa > /dev/null
grep -v '^#' globins.tbl | head -3

Which domains does a protein have? hmmscan

The other way round: one protein against a collection of profiles, as Pfam's are used. The tutorial joins three profiles – globins, fibronectin type III (fn3) and protein kinase – into a small collection, which hmmpress prepares for hmmscan, and scans the Sevenless receptor of Drosophila (7LESS_DROME, a UniProt record, which HMMER reads as it is):

cat /data/examples/hmmer/globins4.hmm /data/examples/hmmer/fn3.hmm /data/examples/hmmer/Pkinase.hmm > minifam.hmm
hmmpress minifam.hmm
hmmscan --domtblout 7less.domtbl minifam.hmm /data/examples/hmmer/7LESS_DROME > 7less.out
grep -v '^#' 7less.domtbl | awk '{print $1, $20, $21, $13}'

Nine fibronectin type III domains and one kinase domain, with where each lies on the protein (columns 20 and 21: from, to) and its E-value (i-Evalue, column 13); a globin, none. Two of the fn3 domains are weak, with E-values of 0.5 and 0.19: a domain like that is a candidate to look at, not a finding. The InterPro card of the Databases tab shows what InterPro, with the whole of Pfam, finds in the same protein (UniProt P13368).

One sequence as the query: phmmer and jackhmmer

phmmer --tblout hbb.tbl /data/examples/hmmer/HBB_HUMAN /data/examples/hmmer/globins45.fa > hbb.out
grep -v '^#' hbb.tbl | head -3
jackhmmer -N 3 /data/examples/hmmer/HBB_HUMAN /data/examples/hmmer/globins45.fa | grep '^@@'

phmmer searches with one protein, as BLASTP does, with HMMER's scores and E-values. jackhmmer searches again and again: it builds a profile from what it found and searches with that, until nothing new comes in – here after two rounds, as all 45 globins are in the first.

DNA: nhmmer

nhmmer --tblout made1.tbl /data/examples/hmmer/MADE1.hmm /data/examples/hmmer/dna_target.fa > made1.out
grep -v '^#' made1.tbl | cut -c1-140

MADE1 is a short DNA transposon of the human genome, from Dfam. nhmmer searches both strands: two copies in this piece of chromosome 1, near 174,500 and 302,400, are each found on both strands, as MADE1 reads almost the same backwards on the other strand (its ends are inverted repeats); the fifth hit, with an E-value above 1, is noise.

From genes to proteins to relatives

The proteins that Prodigal predicted can be searched like any others. E. coli's ThrA, the second gene, against Salmonella's proteins:

awk '/^>/{p=($1==">NC_000913.3_1_60000_2")} p' ecoli.faa > thrA.faa
phmmer --tblout thrA.tbl thrA.faa salmonella.faa > /dev/null
grep -v '^#' thrA.tbl | cut -c1-100
hmmscan --tblout ecoli_domains.tbl minifam.hmm ecoli.faa > /dev/null
grep -v '^#' ecoli_domains.tbl | wc -l

One hit, the first gene of the Salmonella window – its ThrA – with an E-value of 0, too small for the computer to write. None of E. coli's 56 proteins has a globin, fibronectin or kinase domain of these three profiles: the table has no lines but its comments. To annotate a genome, proteins are scanned against all of Pfam's families – more than 20,000 – which is too big for the browser: use the InterPro card, or a computer.

More: hmmalign globins4.hmm globins45.fa aligns sequences to a profile; hmmfetch takes one profile out of a collection, hmmstat lists them, hmmemit writes sequences a profile would produce, hmmconvert converts the format; NAME -h lists a program's options. Here HMMER runs on one thread – --cpu is left out, with a note – and cannot read files compressed with gzip (.gz): unpack them first.

In Galaxy

Galaxy (the Galaxy tab) has Prodigal Gene Predictor and hmmbuild, hmmsearch, hmmscan, phmmer, nhmmer and hmmalign in its Annotation section, which run the same programs.

Prodigal and HMMER are compiled to WebAssembly from their released sources by the genomics-wasm project, and give the same genes, scores and alignments as the same programs built for Linux (see Programs and versions).
