Interactions, pathways and GO

What does a protein do, and with whom? STRING draws the network of its interactions, Reactome names the pathways it takes part in, and the Gene Ontology card lists the standard terms for its function, the processes it is part of, and where it is in the cell.

STRING

STRING collects known and predicted protein–protein associations: from experiments, curated databases, genes that are switched on together (co-expression), genes that sit together in genomes, and papers that mention proteins together (text mining). Type gene or protein names, one per line, choose the species, and Search.

  • Minimum confidence: STRING's combined score, from 0 to 1. high (0.7) keeps the associations it is most sure of.
  • Add neighbours: adds the proteins most strongly connected to yours, to show the neighbourhood.
  • The picture is STRING's own drawing of the network; the table has one row for each pair, with the combined score and its parts (experiments, databases, text mining).

An association is not always a physical contact: two enzymes of one pathway are associated without ever touching. Look at where a score comes from before you call it an interaction.

Reactome

Reactome is a curated database of human biological pathways – reactions, the molecules in them, and how they connect – with predictions for other species. Type a UniProt accession: the answer lists the pathways the protein is in, with Diagram for those that have one; Disease marks pathways of a disease.

Gene Ontology

The Gene Ontology (GO) is a dictionary of terms that describe what genes do, so that all databases say it the same way. It has three parts (aspects):

AspectDescribesExample
Molecular functionwhat the protein does itselfGO:0003700 DNA-binding transcription factor activity
Biological processthe larger process it takes part inGO:0006915 apoptotic process
Cellular componentwhere in the cell it isGO:0005634 nucleus

GO terms of a protein (from QuickGO): type a UniProt accession, and choose an aspect or all three. Each term has an evidence code that says how it was assigned: EXP, IDA, IMP, IPI … from experiments; IBA, ISS from related genes; IEA automatically, without a curator; TAS, NAS from statements in papers. The qualifier says how the protein relates to the term (enables, involved in, located in, part of, or NOT).

Look up a term (from the Ontology Lookup Service) searches the terms of GO and other ontologies: the Human Phenotype Ontology (symptoms and signs), Mondo (diseases), Uberon (anatomy), the Cell Ontology, ChEBI (chemicals), the Sequence Ontology (the names of variant consequences used by VEP).

Lists of genes – the genes changed in an experiment – are compared against GO with an enrichment analysis, which asks which terms occur more often in the list than by chance. In a notebook, the recipe Databases from code asks UniProt about many proteins at once and reads the answer as a table: add go_p, go_f or go_c (biological process, molecular function, cellular component) to the fields of its query, and the table has their GO terms.
